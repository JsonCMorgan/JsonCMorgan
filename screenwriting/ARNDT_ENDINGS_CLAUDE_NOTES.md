# Michael Arndt: Endings — Claude Working Notes for Screenplay Writing

**Source:** Michael Arndt, *Endings: The Good, the Bad, and the Insanely Great*  
**Video:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gWHfsEJ5JJo  
**Also useful:** WGA East OnWriting interview with Arndt about the same lecture  
**Purpose of this file:** Feed this document to Claude (or any writing partner) so it can apply Arndt’s ending mechanics when helping draft, diagnose, or rewrite our screenplay. Treat these as **tools, not rules**. Arndt’s Pixar line: tools not rules. He is describing how three films work, not prescribing the only way to tell a story.

---

## 0. How Claude should use this document

When helping write or rewrite our screenplay:

1. **Do not plot the first draft from this model.** Arndt is explicit: these tools fail if used to paint-by-numbers forward. First get a strong idea, vivid characters, and a messy draft. Then use this to diagnose and strengthen.
2. **Always force clarity on three stakes:** external, internal, philosophical. If any one is muddy, the ending will feel “so what.”
3. **Prefer fairytale compression.** If asked to summarize our story, tell it as: Once upon a time / And every day / But then one day / The hero embarked on a quest / Suddenly without warning / And then there was no going back / Climax.
4. **Judge endings by three tests:** positive, surprising, meaningful. Meaning comes from a value reversal (underdog values overturning dominant values).
5. **Design Act 3 so all three stakes fail together, then flip together** in under ~45–120 seconds of screen time, logically chained.
6. **The decisive act must embody the underdog values.** Do not have the hero give a speech explaining the theme. The action is the theme.
7. When stuck, run the **worksheet at the end** before inventing new plot.

## 1. Core thesis

- Movies are really about the **climactic ~2 minutes**, because that is where meaning is revealed.
- Meaning = the **values** embedded in the story: what is worth living for, what is not.
- Stories often stage **competing visions of how to live**.
- Many scripts set up well, limp through Act 2, then **fail in Act 3**: mechanical, predictable, or “the hero got the girl and the money… so what?”
- Arndt’s fix: treat story as a contest among **three binary stake systems**, then engineer a late reversal where underdog values overturn the moral order of the story universe.

### Ending quality ladder

| Type | Traits |
|------|--------|
| **Bad** | Positive but predictable. You knew it would happen, and it did. |
| **Good** | Positive and surprising. You hoped for it, were unsure how, then boom. |
| **Insanely great** | Positive + surprising + **meaningful**. Meaning is what separates it. |

“Insanely great” also usually requires: total despair → total success in ~45 seconds or less, with three climaxes tightly stacked and ascending in importance.

## 2. The three stakes (remember this first)

Every strong story Arndt analyzes has **three binary, pass/fail stake systems**.

### A. External stakes (outside world)

What can be gained or lost in the tangible world.

Examples:
- Life / survival
- Bag of money / bank job
- Winning a contest
- Status, position, mission success
- Saving the rebellion / blowing up the Death Star
- Olive winning Little Miss Sunshine
- Ben’s future being “different”

### B. Internal stakes (emotional)

What can be gained or lost in the heart / psyche.

Examples:
- Romantic love reciprocated
- Parent–child love / respect (Finding Nemo, Olive ↔ Richard)
- Friendship gained or lost
- Self-respect (Rocky goes 15 rounds even without winning the belt)
- Luke’s call to greatness / destiny
- Ben’s need for real human connection

### C. Philosophical stakes (values contest)

What value system will be proven true by the ending.

Usually framed as:
- **Dominant values** (pervasive, “how the world works,” often spoken by the antagonist)
- **Underdog values** (scrappy, mentor-voiced, often against self-interest)

Examples:
- Community / altruism vs selfish individualism (*Star Wars*)
- Individual feeling vs social conformity / cynicism (*The Graduate*)
- Private-life values (fun, self-definition, love) vs public marketplace values (winners vs losers) (*Little Miss Sunshine*)

**Arndt’s key claim:** almost every movie has external stakes; most have internal stakes; **philosophical stakes are where scripts get muddy or weak**. Theme talk often obscures that what you really need is a clear contest between two value systems, one of which will prevail. That outcome *is* the meaning.

McKee-adjacent point Arndt endorses: **more meaning → more emotion**, especially at the ending.

---

## 3. Dominant vs underdog values (the meaning engine)

1. Establish a **dominant moral order** early (Antagonist’s Aria).
2. Establish **underdog values** via a mentor (or mentor-substitute: feelings, grandpa, etc.).
3. Drive the story so dominant values are **about to win**.
4. Create a **sudden reversal** where underdog values prevail.
5. That reversal **overturns the moral order of the universe** you created. That is the meaning of the story.

In tragedies, the “bad” values may prevail. Same machinery, opposite outcome.

### Special hero type in Arndt’s three examples

Luke, Ben, and Olive are **innocent heroes** in a **flawed universe** (not primarily flawed characters who must be corrected). They become **redeemers of the universe** by enacting underdog values at the climax.

When writing our screenplay, decide early:
- Is our hero flawed-in-a-flawed-world (growth/redemption of self), or
- Innocent-in-a-flawed-world (redeemer of the moral order)?

Arndt’s “insanely great” case studies lean toward the second.

## 4. Story architecture: fairytale / tentpoles

Arndt maps organic narrative logic onto fairy tale language and ~100-page screenplay page counts.

### Fairytale spine

1. **Once upon a time** — who/what/where/when; daily routine; unresolved issue (internal or world-problem). World roughly in equilibrium. Hero has a stable sense of self, world, and future (ideally a clear visual future).
2. **But then one day** — bolt from the blue. Inciting incident. Future changes. Sense of self and world change. Often the worst possible thing for what the hero values most. Often add insult-to-injury for “emotional rocket fuel.” Creates the **global problem** solved at the climax.
3. **The hero embarked upon a quest** — first-act break / blast-off. Long-range goal + short-term plan (Dorothy: home → wizard → yellow brick road).
4. **Suddenly without warning** — midpoint. Rug pulled out. Setback / direction change. Deepen stakes. Reveal hidden info. Force hero to see a previously blind flaw or truth.
5. **And then there was no going back** — second-act break. Trapdoor. Do-or-die. Hero may have achieved the **local/second-act goal**, but that achievement inadvertently forces the **global crisis**.
6. **Climax** — achieve or fail the global goal; resolve all three stakes.

### Tentpole page map (100-page / ~100-minute model)

| Moment | Approx. page | Image / function |
|--------|--------------|------------------|
| Opening | 1 | Tank up emotional rocket fuel |
| Inciting incident | ~10 | Light the fuse; shatter status quo |
| First-act break | ~25 | Blast-off; max rooting interest; quest begins |
| Midpoint | ~50 | Rug pulled out; new way forward |
| Second-act break | ~75 | Trapdoor into canoe toward waterfall; all or nothing |
| Climax | ~90 (last ~10 min; meaning in last ~2) | Decisive act; stake reversals |

**Diagnostic:** If you can name these six tentpole moments clearly, you can usually tell whether you have a story.

### Global vs local goals

- **Global goal:** the big pass/fail question set by the inciting incident, answered at climax.
- **Local / Act 2 goal:** smaller quest that seems to lead toward solving the global problem.
- At the second-act break, achieving the local goal **forces** the global crisis.

Examples:
- *LMS:* Global = win pageant? Local = get the family to Redondo Beach.
- *Star Wars:* Global = survival of the rebellion / stop Death Star. Local = deliver R2 / plans (later: rescue Leia). Escaping Death Star delivers plans **and** reveals the Rebel base.
- *Graduate:* Global = make Ben’s future different. Local/emotional = connect with someone. Connecting with Elaine triggers the crisis that forces the wedding chase.

## 5. Cast functions for the value war

### Antagonist’s Aria (Act 1)

Antagonist (or dominant-value spokesperson) tells the hero: **“Kid, here’s how the world works.”**  
They should sound smart, not cartoon-evil. Their worldview should be literally persuasive for most of the film.

Examples:
- Tarkin: fear / Death Star / empire maintains control
- Mr. Robinson: sew wild oats, don’t take life seriously (surface); deeper dominant order = cynicism, using people, social expectation over love
- Richard Hoover: winners vs losers
- Bonus from interview: Big Lebowski’s “the bums lost”; Potter’s definition of “richest man”

### Mentor speech (Act 1)

Mentor states underdog values: **another way to live**.

Examples:
- Obi-Wan: Force / connection / help each other / democracy over empire
- Ben’s mentor = **his feelings** (no wise elder)
- Grandpa: fun, try, tell them all to go to hell; private self over public judgment

### Philosophical antagonist / ally pressure

Often a close figure embodies or argues the dominant values (Han’s “no mystical energy field”; later Han’s exit as Judas beat).

### Judas moment of betrayal (late Act 2 / early Act 3)

Hero’s **closest ally** chooses dominant values, rejects underdog values.

Examples:
- Han flees Rebel base
- Elaine’s note: “I love you but it would never work” (feelings lose to social rules)
- Dwayne: “She’s not a beauty queen… don’t let her do this” (seeing Olive through winners/losers eyes)

### Kamikaze moment of commitment (~p.89)

Mentor voice returns (literally or as internalized values). Hero chooses underdog values **against self-interest**. Everyone else should think: **“What are you doing? That’s crazy.”** Audience should fear the hero is making it worse.

Examples:
- “Use the Force, Luke… let go”
- Ben pounding glass: “Elaine! Elaine! Elaine!”
- Olive choosing to go onstage after being told she can’t win

### Moment of despair

If you stopped the movie here, audience should say there is **no positive outcome possible**. Hero has failed:
- externally
- internally
- philosophically

The more absolute the despair, the more the happy ending feels earned.

### Decisive act

Positive, surprising, meaningful action that **enacts underdog values**. This flips stakes. Ideally:
1. Philosophical flip (values proven)
2. Enables external flip
3. Enables internal flip  
(or a tightly chained variant; order can vary, but keep them adjacent and causal)

Target: total failure → total success in **~45 seconds or less**, with three climaxes in ascending importance / tight proximity. Hardest craft problem in this model.

## 6. Case study cards (for pattern matching)

### Star Wars

| Stake | Conflict | Failure at despair | Flip |
|-------|----------|--------------------|------|
| External | Rebellion survival vs Empire/Death Star | Vader locks on Luke; base about to die | Han shoots Vader off; Luke hits exhaust port |
| Internal | Call to greatness vs “just a farm boy” (Uncle Owen) | Owen was right; dies like father, no destiny | Obi-Wan: Force will be with you always |
| Philosophical | Altruism/community/Force-connection vs fear, empire, selfishness | Han was “smart” to leave; tyranny/greed win | Han returns as teammate; Luke trusts Force over computer |

**Act machinery notes:** Alderaan destroyed early Act 2 (plan disrupted). Midpoint: sucked into Death Star → new goal rescue Leia. Second-act break: escape succeeds **and** base location revealed. Stacked setbacks: Obi-Wan dies, Han leaves, pilots die, R2 hit, primary ignition, Vader lock-on.

**Interview nuance:** Ending vindicates **both** teamwork (Han) **and** individual trust-your-feelings (Luke ditching targeting computer). Dual value vindication.

### The Graduate

| Stake | Conflict | Failure at despair | Flip |
|-------|----------|--------------------|------|
| External | Ben’s future being different | Elaine married to Carl; empty future sealed | Ben & Elaine escape church |
| Internal | Real connection/love with Elaine | Bond seemingly lost; love not reciprocated in time | Elaine calls “Ben” |
| Philosophical | Live by feelings/individual truth vs rules, conformity, cynicism | Rules/cynicism/conformity triumph | “It’s too late.” / “Not for me.” |

**Notes:** Plastics is a red herring Aria; real Aria is Mr. Robinson’s lifestyle lecture + Mrs. Robinson’s cynical world. Mentor = feelings. One perfect bonding scene with Elaine must hit emotional **and** philosophical connection (rules made by wrong people). Opposite of Star Wars philosophically: individual feeling over community rules.

### Little Miss Sunshine

| Stake | Conflict | Failure at despair | Flip |
|-------|----------|--------------------|------|
| External | Win Little Miss Sunshine? | Olive alone on stage, out of league, about to be humiliated | She tears pants off / dances anyway (**external may stay “loss”**) |
| Internal | Hopes/dreams + fear of being a loser who disappoints Dad | Family shame; dad–daughter bond threatened | Richard converts, stands and supports her |
| Philosophical | Private life (fun, self-definition, try) vs public winners/losers marketplace | Winning > fun; image > private self | Dance enacts Grandpa’s values |

**Design choice:** Keep external stakes **small**, emotional and philosophical stakes **huge** (comedy strategy). Grandpa dies (mentor removed), then returns as internalized voice. Olive can “lose” the contest and still win the ending because philosophical + internal flip.

**Soapbox (why the movie exists):** Public-self values (status, rank, approval) are invading private life and childhood. Private values (love, friendship, creativity, fun) are more within one’s control; Stoic happiness logic.

## 7. Full Act 3 recipe (insanely great ending)

1. **Second-act break reversal:** local goal achieved → global crisis forced.
2. **Act 3 drive:** nothing goes right. External + internal + philosophical setbacks. Include Judas betrayal.
3. **Kamikaze commitment:** hero chooses mentor/underdog values; looks crazy.
4. **Moment of despair:** triple failure; no positive outcome imaginable.
5. **Decisive act:** enacts underdog values; causes rapid multi-stake reversal; overturns moral order of the universe in ~45 seconds.
6. **Resolution:** underdog values visibly prevail (even if literal contest trophy is not won).

---

## 8. Process rules (from lecture + WGA interview)

- **Inspiration first, mechanics second.** Great idea, characters, audacity, dangerous edge. Then drafts.
- Use this model **retrospectively**, when draft 8–10 still isn’t landing.
- “When all else fails, read the directions.”
- Fairytale retelling is a beta test: fluff and weakness become obvious.
- Villain must be **right in the literal sense** for most of the movie. Hero’s epiphany often **redefines a key word** (e.g., “richest man in town” in *It’s a Wonderful Life*).
- How the good guy beats the bad guy matters because **the method of victory is the meaning**.
- This “insanely great” ending is only one subset. Other great endings exist (Ozu *Late Spring*, Ray *Pather Panchali*, Takahata *My Neighbors the Yamadas*, *Pan’s Labyrinth*, transcendental/slow cinema per Schrader). Do not force this template onto every story.

### E. Diagnosis prompts for Claude

Ask Claude to answer against the draft:
1. Where are philosophical stakes first articulated?
2. Can a stranger state Value A vs Value B in one line?
3. Does Act 3 contain a true despair where all three stakes fail simultaneously?
4. Is the decisive act an enactment of underdog values, or just a clever plot solution?
5. Are the three climaxes close enough and causally linked?
6. If we cut the last two minutes, what meaning remains? If none, the ending is carrying the theme correctly; if the theme was already lectured, tighten.

## 11. Quick application recipe for our writing sessions

When we ask Claude to help write scenes:

1. Restate our three stakes and value war in one short block.
2. Identify which tentpole the scene serves.
3. Ensure the scene pressure moves at least one stake toward jeopardy or reversal.
4. For climax scenes: write despair first, then kamikaze choice, then decisive act as behavior (not speech).
5. After drafting, run Section 10E diagnosis and patch the weakest stake system first (usually philosophical).

## 12. One-page cheat sheet

**Insanely great ending =**  
create a flawed universe with clear dominant values →  
put underdog values on the ropes via Act 3 setbacks + Judas betrayal →  
hero kamikaze-commits to mentor values →  
absolute triple despair →  
decisive act enacts underdog values →  
flip philosophical / external / internal stakes in ~45 seconds →  
overturn the moral order of the universe.

**Remember:** describe, don’t prescribe. Tools, not rules. First draft from life and character. Use this when the ending feels mechanical, predictable, or “so what.”
